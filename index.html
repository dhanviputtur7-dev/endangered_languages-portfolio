The project

A closer look at language risk

Why this question?

I grew up speaking Tulu, a language with about 1.7 million speakers that is still classified as Vulnerable. That made me curious: what is associated with endangerment, and does speaker count tell the whole story? This project looks at speaker estimates, world area, and a small sample of how languages are described.

It is intended as a starting point for community language programs and the people who support them.

Dataset & fields

Source: Andy Warui’s Endangered Languages Project dataset on Kaggle. The notebook uses 2,722 rows and selects 10 fields for analysis:

language IDnamecountriescountry codesISO codesendangerment levelspeaker estimatelatitudelongitudelocation description

Each row represents one endangered or extinct language. The map uses 2,719 rows with coordinates.

Status levels in this dataset

From lower to higher endangerment:

VulnerableDefinitely endangeredSeverely endangeredCritically endangeredExtinct

What to keep in mind

  • Only endangered and extinct languages are included; stable languages are absent.
  • Collection date and method are undocumented, and speaker counts come from mixed sources.
  • 183 records have unknown speaker counts; blank is not the same as zero.
  • A single point approximates a language’s location and cannot show its range or movement.
  • The dataset was not collected with language communities and describes a snapshot, not causes or change over time.
  • World-area boundaries are rough longitude-based groupings, so languages near their edges may be assigned imperfectly.
  • Wikipedia coverage favors better documented languages; word searches miss context and meaning.

01 · Map

Where are endangered languages located?

Approximate points appear across three broad world areas, both inside and outside the tropics. Dense clusters do not tell us how many stable languages are nearby.

02 · Size vs place

Similar speaker counts, different risk

Smaller languages are generally more endangered. Among languages with 100–999 speakers, the critically endangered share is much higher in Europe, Africa & W. Asia than in the other areas.

03 · Tropics test

The tropical belt does not explain the pattern

Overall, the shares inside and outside the tropics are nearly the same. The direction changes across world areas, so latitude alone is not a useful explanation.

04 · Wikipedia

Articles offer a small window into language stories

Teaching and revival words appeared in 12 of 20 Americas articles, 7 of 17 in Europe, Africa & W. Asia, and 3 of 19 in Asia & Oceania. This is a small word-search sample, not a measure of meaning.

05 · Tulu

Tulu is vulnerable, with millions of speakers

Tulu is estimated at about 1.7 million speakers, yet remains Vulnerable. It shows why speaker count and endangerment status do not always align neatly.

A possible next experiment

Could conversation help learners continue?

The dataset describes endangerment patterns; it cannot tell us what helps someone keep learning. This small A/B proposal explores one possible support.

Version A

Lessons

Learners use the same language-learning lessons on their own.

Version B

Lessons + conversation

Learners get the same lessons, plus an option to connect with a volunteer speaker.

What would we look for?

Compare the share of new adult learners who complete at least one lesson in week four. The idea is that a real conversation partner may give learners a reason to return. Community approval, learner privacy, and volunteer well-being would guide any test. A future, separate study could explore how children under 18 learn and engage, with age-appropriate methods and community and guardian guidance.

This is a proposal, not a finding from this dataset.
