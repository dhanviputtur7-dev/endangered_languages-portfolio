We tell stories all the time.

Where we come from, what a song means, how we got here. Every dot here is a language people use to tell theirs.

A language can hold a great deal. Tulu, which still has millions of speakers, carries the Epic of Siri, an oral epic that Wikipedia says is nearly as long as the Iliad.

Now imagine the stories that go quiet when the last speaker does. Which ones will we still be able to hear?

Project details

By YOUR NAME · DESIGNTK 530 · Fall 2026

Overview

Question: How are speaker population and geographic location associated with the severity of language endangerment around the world? Audience: community language programs and the groups that support them.

Data: Endangered Languages Project on Kaggle (Andy Warui). One row per endangered or extinct language, with status, estimated speakers, and one map point: 2,722 languages, 2,719 with a location. The collection method is not documented, and stable languages are not included. For the text analysis I used the plain text of 56 Wikipedia articles from a random sample.

Key findings

  • Smaller languages are usually more endangered.
  • At the same size, place still matters: about 55% of languages with 100 to 999 speakers in Europe, Africa & W. Asia are critically endangered, against 13 to 15% elsewhere.
  • The tropics did not predict risk (31.3% vs 31.9%).
  • Wikipedia articles mention teaching and revival more often for the Americas (12 of 20) than Asia & Oceania (3 of 19).

These are patterns, not causes, from one snapshot of endangered languages only.

Proposed A/B test

An app for learning an endangered language. A: lessons only. B: the same lessons plus a way to connect with a volunteer speaker. We expect that adding speaker connections will lead to more learners still doing lessons four weeks after sign-up, because a real person to talk to gives them a reason to come back. Main measure: the share of new learners who finish at least one lesson between day 22 and day 28. Watch: volunteers leaving. Adopt B only if it gains about 10 points (an example, set in advance) and no more than 1 in 5 volunteers leave. A language community must agree first, and volunteers control their own availability.
