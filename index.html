The project

A closer look at language risk

Why this question?

I grew up speaking Tulu, a language with about 1.7 million speakers that is still classified as Vulnerable. That made me curious: what is associated with endangerment, and does speaker count tell the whole story? This project looks at speaker estimates, world area, and a small sample of how languages are described.

It is intended as a starting point for community language programs and the people who support them.

Dataset & fields

Source: Andy Warui’s Endangered Languages Project dataset on Kaggle. The notebook uses 2,722 rows and selects 10 fields for analysis:

language IDnamecountriescountry codesISO codesendangerment levelspeaker estimatelatitudelongitudelocation description

Each row represents one endangered or extinct language. The map uses 2,719 rows with coordinates.

Status levels in this dataset

From lower to higher endangerment:

VulnerableDefinitely endangeredSeverely endangeredCritically endangeredExtinct

Some things to note

  • Only endangered and extinct languages are included; stable languages are absent.
  • Collection date and method are undocumented, and speaker counts come from mixed sources.
  • 183 records have unknown speaker counts; blank is not the same as zero.
  • A single point approximates a language’s location and cannot show its range or movement.
  • The dataset was not collected with language communities and describes a snapshot, not causes or change over time.
  • World-area boundaries are rough longitude-based groupings, so languages near their edges may be assigned imperfectly.
  • Wikipedia coverage favors better documented languages; word searches miss context and meaning.

Questions that shaped the analysis

Does the share of critically endangered or extinct languages differ by area and by tropics?
Does place still matter when I compare languages of the same size?
How do Wikipedia articles about these languages describe them?

What do the visualizations say?

01 · Map

Where are endangered languages located?

Approximate points appear across three broad world areas, both inside and outside the tropics. Dense clusters do not tell us how many stable languages are nearby.

02 · Size vs place

Similar speaker counts, different risk

Smaller languages are generally more endangered. Among languages with 100–999 speakers, the critically endangered share is much higher in Europe, Africa & W. Asia than in the other areas.

03 · Tropics test

The tropical belt does not explain the pattern

Overall, the shares inside and outside the tropics are nearly the same. The direction changes across world areas, so latitude alone is not a useful explanation.

04 · Wikipedia

Articles offer a small window into language stories

Teaching and revival words appeared in 12 of 20 Americas articles, 7 of 17 in Europe, Africa & W. Asia, and 3 of 19 in Asia & Oceania. This is a small word-search sample, not a measure of meaning.

05 · Tulu

Tulu is vulnerable, with millions of speakers

Tulu is estimated at about 1.7 million speakers, yet remains Vulnerable. It shows why speaker count and endangerment status do not always align neatly.

A/B testing

Can conversation help learners keep learning?

This proposed test asks whether a real person to talk to could help new learners keep learning.

What the data suggests

Speaker counts are often small

The middle speaker count in the dataset is about 800. Most endangered languages have very few speakers.

What the data cannot answer

Why do learners continue?

This dataset cannot show why learners stop or what brings them back. The test would explore one possible support.

Assumption & prediction

Conversation may give learners a reason to return

We expect more learners assigned to Version B to complete at least one lesson in their fourth week because they can talk with a real person.

Version A · baseline

Lessons only

An app with the language lessons and no speaker connection feature.

Version B · one change

Lessons + speaker connection

The same app and lessons, plus an option to connect with a volunteer speaker. This is the only difference.

Who takes part?

Eligible participants

  • New learners aged 18 and older.
  • Languages with enough volunteer speakers.
  • Randomly assign learners to A or B at sign-up, separately within each language.
How long?

Run both versions together

  • No switching between versions.
  • Stop sign-ups after 8 weeks.
  • Wait 4 more weeks so everyone has a full fourth week.
Main success measure

Who is still learning in week four?

Percentage of learners who complete at least one lesson in their fourth week. Count everyone who signed up, even if they quit early or never connected with a speaker.

Improvement target: Version B should raise this by at least 10 percentage points. For example, 20 of 100 learners in A and 30 of 100 in B.

A smaller gain may not justify finding and supporting volunteers.

Volunteer safeguard

Watch for overload

Track the share of volunteers who stop taking connections. B should not cause more than 1 in 5 volunteers to leave.

More learner activity is not a success if volunteers, often elders, are overloaded.

Interpret with care

What could mislead us?

  • Some languages have more volunteers; compare A and B within each language.
  • A new feature may be exciting at first; set the end date in advance and do not stop early.
  • Results may not apply where volunteers are scarce, communities opt out, or internet access is limited.
Community first

Consent, privacy, and control

Do not run the test without the language communities’ approval and control over participation and privacy. Keep conversations private.

A future question for younger learners

A separate, community-led study could explore how children under 18 learn and engage differently, using age-appropriate methods and guardian consent.
